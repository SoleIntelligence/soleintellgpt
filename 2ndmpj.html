The Second MTPJ Problem

A joint nobody studies cleanly — and the quiet shift from cutting it out to saving it.

A teaching brief for residents  ·  By Peter Sorensen, DPM
01
Start here

There is no clean "2nd MTPJ osteoarthritis" literature

Primary OA at this joint is rare. If you search "second MTPJ arthritis," you will find almost nothing. The degeneration is real — it just hides under three other diagnoses. Search those instead.

Pathway A · the big one

Freiberg's Infraction

AVN of the metatarsal head. The largest, most surgically relevant body of evidence relevant to this joint. Where most "2nd MTPJ" surgery actually lives.

Pathway B

Instability & Plantar Plate

Crossover toe / predislocation syndrome. The plantar plate fails, the toe deviates, and untreated dislocation ends in secondary DJD.

Pathway C

Trauma · Inflammatory · Iatrogenic

Post-fracture, rheumatoid forefoot, or degeneration that follows a prior Weil osteotomy and transfer metatarsalgia.

02
Why the second ray

The most loaded, least forgiving lesser joint

The second metatarsal is the longest, recessed into the cuneiform mortise as the Lisfranc keystone — stiff where the first ray is mobile. Peak plantar bending load sits beneath it. The plantar plate is the primary static stabilizer, and the 2nd MTPJ lacks a plantar interosseous insertion, leaving it exposed to unopposed lumbrical pull. The result is a joint primed for both overload arthrosis and instability.

03
Staging Freiberg's

The Smillie ladder (1955)

Descriptive, widely cited, and — honestly — inconsistently used. In one survey ~70% of surgeons said the stage did not change their procedure choice. Still the common language, so know it cold. The pivot point is whether the plantar cartilage is still intact.

IFissure fracture in the epiphysis; subtle sclerosis. Often MRI-only. Conservative.
IICentral depression / early collapse of the articular surface.
IIICentral absorption with medial & lateral projections — plantar cartilage still intact. The joint-sparing sweet spot.
IVLoose body; fracturing of the peripheral projections.
VFlattened, deformed head; frank arthrosis; plantar involvement. Joint preservation gets hard.
04
How the approach changed

From resecting the joint to rebuilding it

The arc is unmistakable: a century of drift away from destroying the joint and toward sparing, decompressing, and finally resurfacing it. The osteotomy that rotates healthy plantar cartilage into the load zone was the hinge point; cartilage transplantation is the current frontier.

1914
Freiberg describes the infraction of the 2nd metatarsal head.
1955
Smillie publishes the five-stage radiographic classification.
1979
Gauthier & Elbaz — the dorsiflexory closing-wedge osteotomy (DCWMO), 53 cases. Rotates intact plantar cartilage into the joint. Becomes the workhorse.
1984
Weil begins the distal oblique shortening osteotomy — later borrowed for both Freiberg's and plantar plate repair.
1987
Coughlin defines crossover-toe deformity, launching the instability literature.
1995
Freiberg & Freiberg — core decompression for early, pre-collapse disease.
2008+
Osteochondral autograft (OAT / mosaicplasty) enters foot-and-ankle practice for head resurfacing.
2019
Georgiannos — the field's only RCT: OAT vs DCWMO in athletes.
2026
Meta-analysis (16 studies, 259 toes) tilts toward OAT as the preferred joint-preserving option.
05
The verdict

Joint-sparing outperforms joint-destruction

Schade's 2015 systematic review (Level IV) is the field's anchor synthesis. The signal is consistent even if the evidence is soft: preserve the joint where you can.

Joint-sparing · 257 procedures, mean f/u 30 mo>90%
Joint-destructive · 70 procedures, mean f/u 15 mo>70%

Pain resolution + full return to activity. A second review (2020) graded the evidence for essentially every procedure as poor (grade C) — sparing and sacrificing alike. Read the trend, not the decimals.

06
The surgical menu

What's actually described in the literature

Joint-Sparing / Preserving

Debridement / cheilectomy / synovectomy
Any stage. Low morbidity, often combined or arthroscopic. Doesn't address collapse.
Core decompression
Smillie I–II, pre-collapse. Anecdotal / case-report level.
DCWMO (Gauthier-Elbaz)
Smillie III–IV, plantar surface intact. The most-reported option; 84–100% good results. Costs ~15° MTPJ motion.
Osteochondral autograft (OAT)
Advanced disease, >⅓ surface lost. Knee-plug resurfacing; preserves length. The modern challenger.
Interpositional arthroplasty
Late III–V. EDL / EDB / periosteum-fat / allograft spacer. Best joint-sparing salvage.
Head remodeling · distraction
Case-series and emerging; thin evidence.

Joint-Destructive / Sacrificing

Metatarsal head resection (DuVries)
The old mainstay — largely abandoned for isolated disease. Causes transfer metatarsalgia & instability. Survives in rheumatoid forefoot.
Implant / hemi / total arthroplasty
Silastic & metallic tried; small series (e.g. 63% good, n=31). Newer designs at proof-of-concept. Not standard.
Lesser MTPJ arthrodesis
Rare; mainly for crossover toe, not pure arthritis. Karlock: 10/11 good–excellent. Nonunion ~13%. Niche.

The headline debate

DCWMO vs OAT for advanced Freiberg's

This is the one place the field has comparative data. The lone RCT called them equal; the 2026 meta-analysis leans OAT. The numbers below are the ones worth remembering.

RCT: Georgiannos 2019, n=27, 3-yr follow-up — statistically better AOFAS for OAT, not clinically decisive. Both work. Meta-analysis 2026: AOFAS mean difference +7.15 favoring OAT, complication risk ratio 0.23.

08
The cross-cutting workhorse

The Weil osteotomy — and its tax

The Weil shortens an elongated metatarsal with stable fixation and shows up everywhere: metatarsalgia, instability (with plantar plate repair), and modified for Freiberg's. But it carries a signature complication that every resident must counsel for.

36%
Floating toe across 1,131 Weil osteotomies — the most common complication. Recurrence 15%, transfer metatarsalgia 7%, delayed/non-/malunion ~3%.Highlander et al, systematic review (2011)

Worse when combined with PIP arthrodesis. The triple-cut ("triple Weil") modification aims to reduce plantar translation and the floating-toe rate. Iatrogenic 2nd MTPJ stiffness and degeneration are recognized downstream consequences.

09
Pathway B in depth

The instability cascade → secondary DJD

The plantar plate attenuates, then tears. Sagittal instability becomes transverse deviation — the crossover toe. Left alone, it ends in fixed dislocation and degenerative joint disease. The treatment philosophy moved from indirect balancing to direct anatomic repair.

Then

Indirect

Weil osteotomy alone, or flexor-to-extensor (Girdlestone-Taylor) transfer to rebalance the toe.

Now

Direct Repair

Anatomic plantar plate repair via dorsal (with Weil) or plantar approach. Coughlin-practice series: well-aligned toe in 87%, recurrence 7.6%.

Watch for

The Drawer Sign

The most reliable physical finding for instability (Kaz & Coughlin). Test it before the radiograph convinces you otherwise.

10
Decision lens

How to think through it

1

Diagnose the driver — don't code "arthritis"

Freiberg's (image + Smillie stage), instability (drawer test, MRI), post-traumatic, or inflammatory. This single decision dictates everything downstream.

2

Exhaust non-operative care first

≥6 months for Freiberg's I–III: offloading orthoses, met pads, stiff/rocker sole, NSAIDs, judicious injection (avoid weakening the plantar plate). ~60% of non-athletes get relief.

3

Match procedure to stage & surface

Early (I–II): debridement ± core decompression. Intermediate (III–IV) with intact plantar cartilage: DCWMO, or OAT if >⅓ surface lost and expertise exists. Late (IV–V): interpositional arthroplasty as best sparing salvage; reserve resection/implant/fusion for failures and rheumatoid forefoot.

4

Honor the plan-changers

Plantar surface destroyed → abandon DCWMO, resurface or interpose. Met excessively long → add shortening. Choosing OAT → counsel on the knee. Any Weil → counsel on floating toe and avoid concurrent PIP fusion where possible.

11
Where experts disagree

Controversies & pearls

⚠ Controversies / Debates

  • DCWMO vs OAT — RCT says equal, meta-analysis leans OAT; knee donor-site morbidity debated.
  • Plantar plate repair — indirect vs direct, and dorsal vs plantar approach.
  • Does Smillie stage matter? Most surgeons say it doesn't change their plan.
  • The Weil itself — championed and criticized; much controversy traces to technique drift and concurrent PIP fusion.

✦ Pearls

  • Stage III is the sweet spot — intact plantar cartilage is what DCWMO and interposition both exploit.
  • Length is currency — DCWMO shortens, OAT preserves; weigh it against transfer metatarsalgia risk.
  • The drawer test beats the radiograph for instability.
  • Destruction is a salvage, not a default — head resection lives on mainly in the rheumatoid forefoot.

Read this before you cite anything

How good is the evidence, really?

The whole field rests on Level IV retrospective series — often fewer than 15 patients, no standardized outcome measure, inconsistent staging, short follow-up. There is exactly one RCT (n=27). No procedure is an established gold standard. Treat every percentage here as low-confidence and procedure-specific.

Confidence by claim
Joint-sparing > joint-destructive (the trend)High · consistent
DCWMO vs OAT — which is betterMedium · debated
Implants & lesser-MTPJ arthrodesis for arthritisLow · emerging
