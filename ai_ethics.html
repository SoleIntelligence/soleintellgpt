The Ethical Weight of AI

Five questions every healthcare professional should be able to answer about who owns AI, how it was built, and what it costs the planet.

By Peter Sorensen, DPM Educational brief  ·  Broad healthcare audience

You use it to draft notes, summarize literature, and generate images. But do you know where the data came from? Who owns what you make? Or what it costs the planet every time you hit send? AI is no longer peripheral to healthcare — which means AI ethics is no longer someone else's problem.

1
Training Data & Consent

How AI learned what it knows

Every large AI model was trained on text, images, and code scraped from the internet — including works created by artists, journalists, researchers, and clinicians who were never asked for permission. The legal question of whether that constitutes fair use is still being argued in courts worldwide.
$1.5B Anthropic's copyright settlement, Aug 2025 — largest in U.S. history
Jan 2026 California mandates disclosure of AI training data sources and copyright status
EU 2026 AI Act requires training data summaries and honoring creator opt-outs
→The crux: Most internet content is automatically copyright-protected from the moment it's created. Whether scraping and training on it constitutes infringement hinges on "fair use" — a doctrine courts are still applying unevenly.
→For healthcare: Patient data, clinical notes, and medical literature shared online may have entered training pipelines. HIPAA compliance for AI training data remains an active regulatory question.
→Watch for: Opt-out mechanisms, licensing deals between publishers and AI companies, and mandatory training data transparency — all expanding rapidly in 2026–2027.
2
IP & Ownership

Who owns what you make with AI?

Short answer: probably nobody — at least not yet. The U.S. Copyright Office and courts have consistently held that purely AI-generated content cannot be copyrighted because copyright requires a human author.
Mar 2026 U.S. Supreme Court declined Thaler v. Perlmutter — AI cannot hold copyright
Jan 2025 Copyright Office: AI prompts alone do not satisfy human authorship requirement
→The gray zone: Heavily edited or curated AI output may qualify for protection if human creative input is "significant enough." Courts are still defining what that means.
→What this means practically: An AI-generated clinical summary, educational image, or marketing piece you publish may not be legally yours — or anyone's. Anyone could reproduce it.
→Safe practice: When IP matters, document your creative input. If you directed, edited, and transformed AI output substantially, that human layer may support copyright registration. Disclose AI use when registering.
3
Bias & Fairness

Garbage in, garbage out — at scale

AI models learn from human-generated data — which means they inherit human biases. In healthcare, that's not an abstract problem. A model trained predominantly on data from certain demographics will underperform for others.
→Known examples in medicine: Pulse oximeters overtriaged white patients due to skin-tone bias. Dermatology AI models showed lower accuracy for darker skin types. Pain assessment tools showed race-based disparities.
→In podiatry specifically: Diabetic foot and wound AI tools trained on non-diverse populations may misclassify severity in underrepresented groups — a high-stakes error.
→The fix isn't simple: Bias mitigation requires diverse training data, rigorous real-world validation, and transparent reporting of model demographics. Most commercial tools don't provide this yet.
→Your role: Ask vendors what populations a clinical AI tool was validated on before using it. If they can't answer, that's the answer.
4
Image Generation & Artist Rights

The artist didn't consent

Image generators like Midjourney, DALL-E, and Stable Diffusion were trained on billions of images scraped from the internet — including the life's work of illustrators, photographers, and medical artists. Many were scraped without consent, compensation, or even notification.
→What's settled (legally): The output of image generators generally belongs to no one under current U.S. law. Courts have rejected AI authorship claims across the board.
→What's contested: Whether training on copyrighted images constitutes infringement is still litigated. Getty Images, artists' coalitions, and record labels have all filed suit. Universal Music Group settled with AI audio generator Udio in late 2025.
→Emerging norms: Opt-out systems, licensing deals (Disney × OpenAI's Sora, Getty × generative platforms), and royalty frameworks are being negotiated now — they will define this space.
→Healthcare application: Medical illustration, surgical diagrams, and patient education materials created with AI exist in a legal gray zone. Attribute human input clearly when publishing.
5
Environmental Cost

The hidden cost of every prompt

Every AI query consumes energy and water. Data centers running AI workloads require massive cooling systems — and those systems are drawing from freshwater supplies at a scale that was barely discussed three years ago.
~NYC AI's estimated 2025 carbon footprint — equivalent to New York City's annual emissions
764B L Upper estimate of AI's 2025 water footprint — comparable to global bottled water consumption
+7.1% U.S. residential electricity prices in 2025 — data centers a significant contributing factor
→Scale context: By 2030, AI data centers could emit 24–44 million metric tons of CO₂ annually and drain water equivalent to the household usage of 6–10 million Americans.
→The transparency gap: Tech companies rarely disclose indirect water consumption — which can be 4× higher than direct use. Regulatory pressure for environmental disclosure is growing.
→Healthcare angle: Hospitals pursuing sustainability certifications must now consider AI energy use as part of their environmental footprint. This is an emerging area of institutional policy.
The Guidewire take

"AI's benefits in healthcare are real — but so are the costs. The clinicians, institutions, and systems that will use AI ethically are those who understand what it's built on, what it legally produces, who it serves well, and what it takes from the planet. Informed use is not optional. It's professional responsibility."

Clinical pearls
  • Training data consent is the foundation of AI legitimacy — ask vendors where their data came from before using clinical AI tools.
  • AI-generated content (text, images) has no clear copyright owner under current U.S. law. Document your human creative input if IP matters to you.
  • Bias is baked in. Validate AI tools on populations that match your patient panel. "FDA cleared" ≠ validated for your demographic.
  • Image generators were trained largely without artist consent. The ethics aren't resolved — use them with that awareness.
  • Every AI query has an environmental cost. That doesn't mean don't use it — it means use it with intention, not as a default.
