AI × podiatric medical education

AI is rewriting podiatric education

The frontier is small. That's our advantage.

PS
Peter Sorensen, DPM
Nov 2, 2026 · 8 min read · 10 sources

Every podiatry student with a phone already has an AI model in their pocket. The AAMC cites studies finding that as many as 86% of undergraduates have used AI for schoolwork, and medical schools now plan their AI teaching around students who have used it but were never taught it.3 Our students are building habits with these tools right now, between lectures, mostly on their own.

The early research says those habits matter. Depending on how AI is built and used, it can make learners sharper or leave them weaker when the tool is gone. Podiatry has published almost nothing on how our own students learn with it.

I think that gap is an opening. We're small enough to study this as a whole profession and close enough to act on what we find.

Medical school was built for scarce knowledge

For about a century, medical school sold access to knowledge that was hard to get. You sat in the lecture hall because that's where the information was. Exams checked whether you had stored it.

That knowledge now answers back, in seconds and in full sentences, on every phone in the lecture hall. Some of those sentences are wrong. Telling which ones is now part of the job.

Knowledge still matters. A surgeon still has to know the anatomy cold, partly so they can tell when the machine is wrong.

Medicine has already moved

The rest of medicine is already building AI into practice and training.

The AMA surveyed 1,692 physicians in January and February 2026. 72% said they use at least one AI tool in their practice, up from 38% in 2023. Another 9% know AI is in their practice but aren't sure which tools, which is how the AMA reaches its headline figure of 81%.1,2 These numbers are self-reported, and the AMA notes its questions changed between survey waves, so the size of the jump is approximate.

On the teaching side, 77% of the MD and DO schools that answered the AAMC's 2023–24 Curriculum SCOPE Survey said they cover AI.3

Physicians are also worried about what AI does to skills. 88% have at least some concern that AI use will cost doctors skills, and the worry runs higher among physicians in their first 10 years of practice. 70% are very or somewhat concerned about skill loss in current medical students and residents. And 92% want more education and training on AI.2

The evidence points both ways

Two studies from 2025 show the range.

AI can build skill

Kestin and colleagues ran a randomized crossover trial with 194 students in a Harvard physics course.5 Each student learned some material in a strong active-learning class and some from an AI tutor. The team built the tutor on GPT-4 and loaded it with pre-written, step-by-step solutions so it would walk students through problems and stay accurate.

Median test scores started at 2.75. After the class they were 3.5. After the AI tutor they were 4.5. That's a gain of 1.75 points against 0.75 (p < 10⁻⁸), and the tutor group got there in a median of 49 minutes, against about 60 minutes of class time.

The limits: one course at one school, material students were seeing for the first time, and short-term tests that didn't count toward grades. The authors say the result may not hold for complex synthesis or higher-order critical thinking.

Or wear it down

Budzyń and colleagues studied 4 endoscopy centers in Poland that started using AI to help spot polyps during colonoscopy in late 2021.6 They compared colonoscopies done without AI in the 3 months before AI arrived and the 3 months after, across 1,443 patients.

The adenoma detection rate (the share of colonoscopies that find at least one precancerous polyp) fell from 28.4% to 22.4% when these doctors worked without AI. That's an absolute drop of 6.0 percentage points (95% CI 1.6 to 10.5; p = 0.009). With AI switched on, their rate was 25.3%.

The study is observational: it shows unaided detection falling after 3 months of working with AI, and it can't prove the AI caused the fall.

How it's built decides

These studies used different tools in different settings, so neither cancels the other out. Together they show that AI can move learners in either direction, and that how it's built and used decides which way.

My read: the Harvard tutor was built to make students do the reasoning. The colonoscopy AI did part of the looking for the doctors. When a tool does the cognitive work, people get less practice at it. That's my interpretation, not a finding from either paper, and it's the kind of question podiatry could test.

In our own field, accuracy depends on the task

The foot and ankle evidence shows the same spread. Results swing by task and by model.

Reading X-rays. A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 studies found deep-learning models caught 93.2% of foot and ankle fractures and correctly cleared 94.5% of normal films.8 The authors still call for larger studies, outside validation, and testing in real clinical use.

Coding single procedures. Glenn and colleagues asked 5 public AI models for the CPT code (the billing code for a procedure) on 21 common foot and ankle procedures, 4 times each.7 The best model got 92.9% right. The worst got 48.2%. The authors concluded that none are reliable enough to use without careful human checking.

Coding whole visits. In our own poster, ChatGPT 5.5 coded 300 consecutive outpatient podiatry encounters. It coded the whole visit correctly 44.7% of the time, even with tightly engineered prompts.9 That's a conference poster and hasn't been through journal peer review.

The same student can get a near-perfect answer on one task and a wrong answer about half the time on the next. Knowing where the tool is strong and where it fails is a clinical skill, and it can be taught.

Podiatry has almost no research on this

Depending on the search terms, PubMed returns somewhere between 7 and 20 records on podiatry, AI, and education. The broadest of those searches, run on November 2, 2026, returned 14 records. None of them study how DPM students or residents learn with AI. Several aren't about podiatry at all.

That leaves our students learning these tools with no podiatry-specific evidence on how to teach them. It also means whatever we study next becomes the evidence base.

Students will learn AI either way

The open question is whether that learning is designed or accidental.

Designed

Taught on purpose
Guarded gains
A tutor built to make students reason can outperform strong teaching (Kestin 2025).
Verification as a habit
Every output gets treated as a hypothesis to check.
Earned independence
AI use grows with competence, the way operative autonomy already does.

Accidental

Picked up alone
Answers without reasoning
Copying an output skips the effort where learning happens.
Quiet deskilling
Unaided performance slips while assisted work looks fine (Budzyń 2025).
Confident errors
Fluent answers that are wrong or incomplete get accepted at face value (Sorensen 2026).

Parity matters here too. Podiatry has spent decades showing that its training stands beside MD and DO training. As AI literacy becomes an expectation across medicine, silence on it becomes a visible gap.

APMA has stated where the profession stands.10

Podiatric physicians “must be represented in the design, deployment, and regulation of AI use” in health care.

APMA · March 2026

Why our size helps

Large systems change slowly because every change has to pass through a lot of people. A profession our size can try an idea, see what happens, and adjust while larger fields are still forming committees.

We know each other. There are 11 colleges of podiatric medicine.4 That's 55 possible pairings between schools. Ideas spread by conversation.

Fewer layers. There are fewer committees between a good idea and a classroom. A small field can move as one.

Graded autonomy is already our model. Surgical training runs on watch, assist, perform. AI use in training can follow the same steps.

We can study the whole profession. A field this size can measure every school at once. With the literature this thin, whatever we learn becomes the reference point for everyone after us.

The tradeoff is real. Small programs have thin budgets and few faculty with AI expertise. That's an argument for doing this together, across schools.

Where to start

These are my suggestions. None of them have been studied in podiatry yet.

If you're a student or resident: work the problem before you ask the model, then compare. Check every output against a primary source. Keep a running list of where it has been wrong for you.

If you teach: decide in writing where AI is allowed, where it's off, and why. Grade the verification along with the answer. Let access grow with demonstrated competence, the same way OR autonomy does.

If you do research: the basic questions are open. How do DPM students use AI to study? Does unaided performance drop after a year of AI-assisted clinic? Does any of it change board scores? One school can run the pilot. All 11 can replicate it.

The frontier is small

Medicine will spend the next decade working out how to train clinicians alongside software that knows more facts than they do. Someone will work it out first.

Small enough to figure it out first. Close enough to do it together.

If you teach podiatry students or residents, I'd like to hear what you're seeing in your program. And send this to someone who teaches.
