For residency applicants Board outcome reporting
Board Pass Rates:
A Resident's Guide
A look at ABFAS pass-rate reporting. Peter Sorensen, DPM

ABFAS publishes national exam pass rates. Applicants could also benefit from a consistent way to understand outcomes at the programs they are considering.

For students comparing residencies, board outcomes are one useful question among many. Here is what the public ABFAS data show, where the limits are, and how program-level reporting could be done responsibly.

Three questions for applicants

Start with what each number measures

A board pass rate needs context. Which exam? Which graduates? First attempt or eventual completion? Those details determine whether two numbers can be compared.

QUESTION 01
The certification pathway

Qualification and certification happen at different times

ABFAS qualification includes Part I examinations in Foot Surgery and, when applicable, Reconstructive Rearfoot/Ankle Surgery. Certification follows a separate Case Review process. A recent graduate's qualification result and a former graduate's certification result describe different points in that pathway.

AskDoes the rate cover Part I qualification, Case Review, or eventual board certification?
CheckWhich graduation years and examination attempts are included?
QUESTION 02
The public data

National results give context

ABFAS's public pass-rate page shows results by residency graduation class for its Part I examinations and Case Review. The charts help readers see national patterns. They do not identify individual residency programs, so they cannot answer how graduates of a particular program performed.

Useful forUnderstanding the national result for a defined exam and class year.
Limited forComparing the programs on an applicant's rank list.
QUESTION 03
The program conversation

Ask for a defined outcome, with context

Applicants can ask programs how recent graduates progressed through qualification and certification, how many graduates are in the group, and what support residents receive for exam preparation. A useful answer names the denominator, the time period, and the exam. It also leaves room to discuss operative experience, teaching, mentorship, and graduate practice.

A fair question"How do you track your graduates' board outcomes, and what have you changed in response?"
A fair readingOne percentage does not describe the full quality of a residency.

A useful distinction
ITE data

In-training results are related, but separate

ABFAS says residency directors can use Podiatry Residency Resource (PRR) to compare their program's in-training examination (ITE) performance with other programs. That report can help programs assess resident learning.

An ITE comparison is not a report of graduates' board qualification or certification outcomes. Any applicant-facing board outcome report would need its own definitions, review, and privacy safeguards but would be way more useful than what currently exists.

A reasonable next step

How reporting could help

I would support a conversation among programs, CPME, ABFAS, and applicants about a shared format for graduate outcomes with the intent to give students comparable information that is actually useful.

Define the measure

Report qualification and certification separately. Name the exam, graduation years, numerator, denominator, and whether the rate is based on first attempts or eventual completion.

Protect interpretation

Use multiple years where appropriate. Suppress or combine very small groups so readers cannot identify individuals or overread a few results.

Give program context

Let programs describe exam preparation and changes they have made when results suggest a need. This would just help raise the bar for everyone. Which imo we kinda need to do.

Keep the full picture

Board outcomes belong alongside case experience, supervision, teaching, resident support, and graduates' practice paths. But a program that doesn't have consistent pass rates or doesn't act on low pass rates is not one I'd want to train at.

Increased transparency about board pass rates can only help raise the bar for the profession. A rising tide raises all ships, as they say.
