Six Companies Just Got a Seat at the APMA Table

The new APMA Corporate Council is framed as collaboration. Here's who's in the room, what they sell, and what residents should be watching for.

Peter Sorensen, DPM | May 2026 | Analysis
What this is

On May 5, 2026, APMA announced the APMA Corporate Council — a new "forum" of six founding industry members described as thought leaders who will "help raise awareness of critical issues" and engage in "strategic collaboration" with APMA leadership. The founding members paid for access. What they paid, and what they get in return, is not publicly disclosed.

The Six Founding Members — And What They Sell
Amgen
Big Pharma · Biologics
Global biopharma giant. Relevant products include Enbrel (inflammation/RA, foot manifestations) and Repatha (cardiovascular, PAD risk). Amgen's diabetes pipeline is newer and expanding.
Interest: DPM prescribing habits, formulary positioning, PAD/diabetes overlap patients
Vertex Pharmaceuticals
Specialty Pharma
Known for cystic fibrosis (Trikafta). Expanding into pain and inflammatory conditions. Less obvious fit for podiatry — but their expansion into musculoskeletal/pain pipeline is ongoing.
Interest: Pain management policy, emerging indications, specialist access
NextGen Healthcare
EHR / Practice Management
APMA's officially endorsed EHR. Already a corporate partner. The Council represents a deeper seat at the table — beyond endorsement into policy dialogue.
Interest: Market lock-in, AI documentation tools, data access from DPM practices
PICA (ProAssurance)
Malpractice Insurance
Pre-existing relationship
The podiatry malpractice insurer. Founded by podiatrists, endorsed by APMA for 40+ years. Probably the most aligned of the six — but still a vendor with financial interests in scope-of-practice debates.
Interest: Scope expansion = more DPMs to insure; more procedures = higher premiums
Sagis Diagnostics
Podiatric Pathology Lab
Houston-based CAP-accredited lab specializing in podiatric dermatopathology, bone, and soft tissue pathology. Already markets aggressively to DPMs. Already sponsors resident education.
Interest: Specimen referral volume, lab exclusivity, resident brand loyalty early in training
Clutch Medical
Medical Devices / Implants
Foot and ankle surgical device company — fixation systems, implants. Smaller company; DPM surgeons are a core customer base. Council membership = direct access to APMA policy discussions on surgical scope.
Interest: Surgical scope expansion, implant volume, device training programs
How "Corporate Councils" Actually Function in Medical Associations
01
The entry point is money, not expertise.
APMA's existing corporate member program starts at $2,500 and goes to $125,000+. The Corporate Council is a tier above that — offering "exclusive" access to leadership dialogue. The price of a seat is not disclosed publicly. This is standard for medical associations; it is not inherently corrupt. But it is not charity.
02
The formal benefit is "alignment around shared goals."
APMA's own language: council members engage in "joint problem-solving," "strategic collaboration," and help "raise awareness of critical issues." Translation: they get to frame what the problems are and what solutions look like — before the membership ever hears about it.
03
There is no disclosed conflict-of-interest firewall.
APMA has not published governance language describing how Corporate Council input is separated from clinical guidance, advocacy positions, or HOD resolutions. In other specialties, industry-association entanglement has directly shaped CPG recommendations and scope-of-practice advocacy — sometimes in members' interests, sometimes not.
04
This is not new or unique to APMA.
AMA, AAOS, ACS, and most major specialty societies have industry advisory structures. The question isn't whether corporate partnerships exist — they always have. The question is whether members know what they are, and whether transparency is sufficient.

The Honest Tension
Reasons for Concern
No disclosed firewall between council input and APMA clinical/advocacy positions.
Members don't know what was paid or what was promised.
Some members (Sagis, Clutch) directly profit from DPM practice volume — scope decisions affect them financially.
Residents and students are the most downstream from these dynamics — least voice, most affected long-term.
Reasons Not to Panic
This is how every major medical association funds operations. APMA is not an outlier.
Some companies (PICA, Sagis) have genuine alignment with DPM interests — the relationship isn't inherently adversarial.
Industry partnerships have produced real wins: NextGen EHR is genuinely useful; PICA actually insures DPMs well.
Sunlight is the disinfectant — the existence of the council is public. Governance gaps can be addressed through HOD resolutions.
What to Watch — As a Resident
01
Track APMA advocacy positions over the next 12–18 months. If positions on skin substitutes, surgical scope, or lab policy shift in ways that benefit specific council members, note the correlation.
02
Watch HOD resolutions for fingerprints. Industry-aligned priorities often surface as HOD resolutions before becoming policy. That's where your delegate voice matters most.
03
Be aware of who sponsors your education. Sagis, PICA, and Clutch Medical all market directly to residents through CE, lab sponsorships, and cadaver labs. That's not wrong — but it's not neutral either. Know when you're in a sales environment.
04
Ask APMA for the governance policy. A reasonable member demand: publish the conflict-of-interest firewall governing Corporate Council input into advocacy and clinical guidance. This is a legitimate HOD resolution if one doesn't exist.
05
Don't assume conflict — look for evidence. Reflexive cynicism is as useless as naïve trust. The Corporate Council might produce genuinely useful collaboration. Hold the question open and watch what actually happens.
Pearls
💡
Corporate partnerships don't corrupt associations automatically. Undisclosed corporate partnerships do.
🎯
Your HOD delegate is the only formal channel you have to ask APMA governance questions on the record. Use it.
🔍
Companies that profit from DPM volume have structural reasons to support scope expansion — and structural reasons to resist anything that shrinks it.
📋
The resident and student perspective is almost never in the room when these partnerships form. That's not an accident. That's a reason to engage.
