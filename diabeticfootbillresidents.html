Bill Brief · 119th Congress

The paperwork rules behind diabetic foot care are being rewritten right now.

A diabetic foot bill is moving through Congress. It pairs Medicaid physician-recognition parity for DPMs with a Medicare diabetic shoe documentation rewrite, plus a Stark/self-referral conforming amendment. Here is what HR 7905 / S 4070 actually changes, and why the people with the most career ahead of them should be paying the most attention.

01 / WHAT THIS IS

A bipartisan bill with two main policy changes

The Diabetes Foot Health Access and Modernization Act was introduced in both chambers on March 12, 2026. It is bicameral, bipartisan, and led by APMA.

The Senate version is led by Todd Young (R-IN) and Raphael Warnock (D-GA). The House version is led by six members, four of them physicians: John Joyce, MD (R-PA), Diana DeGette (D-CO), Mike Rulli (R-OH), Kim Schrier, MD (D-WA), Mike Kennedy, MD (R-UT), and Darren Soto (D-FL).

It has two main policy changes and one important program-integrity conforming amendment: it writes podiatric physician services into Medicaid as covered physician services, it modernizes Medicare documentation rules for diabetic therapeutic shoes, and it adds the shoe benefit to the Stark/designated-health-services framework.

02 / WHY IT MATTERS TO YOU SPECIFICALLY

You have the longest exposure to whatever gets locked in

Attendings live with these rules for a decade. You live with them for thirty-plus years.

The reimbursement and documentation environment set in your training years is the one you bill under for an entire career. Residents and students carry the most downstream risk from policy decisions made today, and usually have the least say in them.

One of this bill's main provisions is pure billing and documentation mechanics. A second is a Stark/self-referral program-integrity change. That is the part of practice residency trains least and the part that quietly decides whether your future clinic stays solvent. The Medicaid provision is a parity fight that has run for over a decade. Reading this bill is a low-cost way to see how organized podiatry actually moves, before it is your turn to move it.

03 / THE FRAMEWORK

The two policy changes, plus the Stark amendment

01

Modernize the therapeutic shoe documentation rule

Now Medicare generally requires the clinician managing the patient's diabetes to certify need and maintain supporting documentation, while the podiatrist or other qualified practitioner may prescribe and furnish the shoes. That separate documentation chain can delay access to a covered benefit.

Bill Rewrites 1861(s)(12) so that one physician (defined under 1861(r), which includes DPMs) handles all three pieces: documents qualifying findings (neuropathy, ulceration history, foot deformity, prior lower-extremity amputation, or poor circulation), attests to the diabetes diagnosis and comprehensive plan of care, and attests that the patient needs the shoes. Shoes are then fitted and furnished by a physician or other qualified individual such as a pedorthist or orthotist. This collapses the historical dual-certification pathway into a single physician's documentation and attestation.

Effective for items and services furnished on or after Jan. 1, 2028.

02

Add the shoe benefit to Stark/self-referral guardrails

Now Diabetic therapeutic shoes are not on the Stark designated-health-services (DHS) list, so referrals for them have not been governed by the physician self-referral framework that covers most other DHS items.

Bill Adds extra-depth shoes with inserts and custom molded shoes with inserts to the DHS list under the physician self-referral/Stark statute (1877(h)(6)). Adding shoes to the DHS list extends a self-referral restriction; it does not create a new pathway. The policy rationale is not in the bill text itself. Read APMA's redline for context.

03

Medicaid parity for podiatric physicians

Now Podiatric physicians are not guaranteed recognition as physicians under Medicaid (Title XIX), so coverage of DPM services varies by state.

Bill Amends Title XIX so physician services include both physicians under Medicare section 1861(r)(1) and podiatric physicians under section 1861(r)(3), establishing Medicaid coverage parity.

Effective Jan. 1, 2026, with extra time for states that require legislation.

04 / KEY TERMS

Five terms worth knowing cold

Therapeutic shoe benefit
Medicare coverage for diabetic extra-depth or custom molded shoes and inserts that reduce ulceration and amputation risk. Coverage exists. The friction is in the paperwork to access it.
Title XVIII vs Title XIX
Title XVIII is Medicare. Title XIX is Medicaid. This bill touches both: shoe documentation under Medicare, physician parity under Medicaid.
Medicaid parity
Recognizing DPM services as physician services under federal Medicaid rules by incorporating the Medicare podiatric physician definition, rather than leaving access dependent on state-by-state treatment of podiatry benefits.
HELLPP Act
The long-running Helping Ensure Life- and Limb-Saving Access to Podiatric Physicians Act. Earlier HELLPP Act efforts also centered on Medicaid recognition of podiatric physicians and diabetic shoe access. The parity fight is years old; this bill carries it forward.
eAdvocacy
APMA's tool for emailing your member of Congress in about two minutes. There are separate physician, student, and patient versions.
05 / DON'T GET THIS WRONG

Three things people confuse

01
This is not the same bill as the Promoting Access to Diabetic Shoes Act (HR 1616 / S 1805). That one is narrower and focuses on allowing nurse practitioners and physician assistants to satisfy the Medicare shoe documentation requirement. HR 7905 / S 4070 is broader and adds Medicaid parity plus a Stark/self-referral conforming amendment.
02
Introduced is not passed. A bill referred to committee is early-stage, and most bills die there. Treat this as a live effort, not a done deal.
03
The Medicaid parity provision is the heavy lift, and it predates this bill. Reading the headline as a simple shoe fix misses where the real structural change sits.
