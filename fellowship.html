Dossier · Fellowship Architecture

A profession quietly built a second residency—and forgot to write the rules.

Podiatric surgery fellowships have grown more than five-fold since 2012. The fraction subject to meaningful accreditation has barely moved. Three credentialing tiers now coexist with no shared curriculum, no centralized application, no published outcomes data, and no match. This brief maps the entire fellowship universe and asks the question the profession has avoided in print: are we training subspecialists, or papering over a saturated job market?

§ 02 · Architecture

Most residents do not realize the credentialing tier of the program they are applying to. Most fellowship marketing materials do not clarify it.

19
Programs

CPME-Accredited

CPME 820 / 830 · Effective July 1, 2023 · Renamed "Accredited" July 1, 2025

The only tier carrying delegated authority from APMA. Sponsored by hospital, academic health center, or college of podiatric medicine—surgery centers and private practices may co-sponsor only. Director must be ABFAS or ABPM board-certified with ≥3 years post-residency experience. Site visits required. Annual reporting mandatory. Non-competes and productivity-contingent compensation explicitly prohibited.

Critical gap: CPME 820 does not prescribe case minimums or a standardized curriculum. The accreditation is structural, not educational. This is the core of the Armstrong/ABPM critique.

§ 03 · The Debate

The fellowship-proliferation debate is rarely framed cleanly. Below: the strongest version of each side, stated without polemic. Structural arguments, not advocacy positions.

The case for fellowship growth

  • Subspecialization parity. Orthopaedics has formal F&A subspecialty training. Podiatry's surgical scope has expanded faster than residency duration has accommodated.
  • Advanced technique exposure. Total ankle, complex deformity, limb salvage, and pediatric reconstruction routinely exceed what a 36-month residency covers in volume.
  • Market differentiation. Fellowship-trained signals quality to hospital privileging committees and referring orthopaedists in competitive markets.
  • Academic pipeline. Most podiatric surgery faculty positions at academic centers list fellowship as preferred or required.
  • Mentorship density. A year of focused exposure to a high-volume mentor can compress a decade of practice-level learning.
  • Research output. Fellowship years generate the manuscripts and case series that move evidence forward in narrow subspecialty domains.

The case against proliferation

  • Credential inflation. 5× growth in 14 years has shifted fellowship from subspecialization signal toward de facto floor in competitive markets.
  • Workforce concentration. Fellowship gravity pulls 60–80 grads/year into already-saturated FL, CA, OH, PA metros—reinforcing the very saturation problem fellows are trying to escape.
  • Residency Band-Aid. Growth correlates with—and may mask—uneven case volumes across the ~200 podiatric residency programs. Fix residencies; fellowships shrink.
  • Hidden remediation pathway. Plausible but unproven: fellowships may absorb graduates who lack volume to credential. ABFAS does not publish stratified pass rates by fellowship status.
  • Quality dilution. Two-thirds of programs operate without site visits, case audits, or curriculum mandates. The label "fellowship-trained" no longer reliably signals what it used to.
  • Cheap labor risk. $60–75K stipends function as discounted associate hires; opportunity cost vs. first-year associate salary is $50–120K foregone.
"

Fellowships are not the problem. They are a symptom of an overcrowded job market. CPME shouldn't even approve foot and ankle surgery fellowships, since they are duplicative of what residency is supposed to provide.

Position attributed in public commentary to David G. Armstrong, DPM, MD, PhD · USC Keck School of Medicine · paraphrased from podiatry forum discussion

The orthopaedic mirror: AOFAS solved this.

Recent policy activity—and its limits.

§ 07 · Policy Movement
Confirmed
CPME 820 + 830 revised October 2022, effective July 1, 2023. Sponsorship requirements tightened (hospital/AHC required); director requirements strengthened (BC + 3 yr); non-competes and productivity-based pay explicitly prohibited. Case minimums not added.
Confirmed
Terminology change July 1, 2025: "CPME-approved" → "CPME-accredited." Aligns nomenclature with ACGME standard. Substantive standards unchanged.
Confirmed
CPME Conflicts of Interest list publicly released 2025. Response to long-running APMA HOD criticism. Content and scope of disclosures pending broader analysis.
Contested
APMA HOD 2025 Proposition I-25 sought to subordinate CPME to HOD direction on residency, fellowship, and CE standards. ABFAS opposed publicly; CPME issued formal Impact Statement opposing. Outcome: not adopted.
In Progress
APMA HOD 2024 Proposition 2-24 ("One Board"). Seeks ABFAS/ABPM merger. Passed Policy Review with "Do Pass" recommendation. Reduces credentialing balkanization that drives fellowship demand. Ultimate adoption unclear.
Not Happening
No fellowship moratorium enacted. CPME continues approval pace—5 fellowship evaluations completed in 2025. No published plan to add case minimums in next revision cycle (~2029). The structural gap remains open.

Verdict

Fellowship growth is not the disease—it's the symptom.

The strongest version of the pro-fellowship case—subspecialization parity, advanced technique exposure, mentorship density, academic pipeline—is real and defensible for the 19 CPME-accredited programs and a meaningful subset of the ACFAS-Recognized tier. The strongest version of the critic case—workforce concentration, credential inflation, cheap labor, residency Band-Aid—is also real and defensible for the broader middle and unaccredited tail.

The most damning observation is structural: the orthopaedic foot & ankle world has functionally identical training challenges and has solved them through a unified match, a published curriculum, a single oversight body, and routine outcomes research. Podiatry has none of these. Sixteen years after Roukis's 2008 editorial called for university-based, two-year, accredited fellowships, his recommendations remain unmet—while program counts have quintupled. That is governance failure, not professional progress.

The single intervention that would settle the most contested question—are fellowship-trained DPMs passing ABFAS at higher or lower rates than residency-only graduates?—requires only that ABFAS publish data it already collects. The continued unavailability of stratified pass-rate data is the empirical scandal at the center of this debate.

What the HOD should push for: (1) mandatory fellowship case minimums in the next CPME 820 revision (~2029); (2) a centralized application and match comparable to SF Match—the single biggest structural import from the orthopaedic side; (3) public stratified ABFAS pass rates by fellowship status.
