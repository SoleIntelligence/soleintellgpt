Interstate Licensure

A Compact of Their Own

Podiatry finally gets an interstate licensure compact. The eligibility rules are where the story is.

By Peter Sorensen, DPM · Nine-minute read

The Interstate Podiatric Medical Licensure Compact — drafted by the Federation of Podiatric Medical Boards, modelled on the physicians' compact, and endorsed by the American Podiatric Medical Association — is real, funded, and moving through state legislatures. Two states have now enacted it; it is not yet operational anywhere. For the residents and students who stand to gain most from it, the compact's eligibility rules contain a quiet barrier most will not see coming.

· · ·

Who should care, and why.

A short answer for the impatient, before the long one for the careful.

For practicing podiatrists, the compact promises faster cross-state licensure, legitimate multistate telemedicine, and a viable locum-tenens market. For residents and students, it reshapes the second through tenth year of practice — but only for those who clear the compact's eligibility threshold, which is set at specialty board certification. The structural details, set out below, are where the implications live.

· · ·

The five rooms.

A reader new to the alphabet soup of podiatric governance should keep these straight; they are easily confused, and the confusion is consequential.

FPMB
The federation of state podiatric licensing boards. Drafted the compact. Owns the model legislation.
APMA
The membership and advocacy organisation. Endorsed the compact. Lobbies the state-by-state bills.
NBPME
Writes the licensing examination (APMLE Parts I, II, and III).
CPME
Accredits podiatric schools and residency programmes.
ABFAS & ABPM
The specialty board-certification bodies.
IMLCC
The physicians' compact commission. Lent FPMB its administrative architecture under a formal memorandum of understanding.

FPMB built the compact. APMA is selling it. The physicians' compact loaned the blueprint. The remaining bodies feed credentials into the eligibility filter.

· · ·

Not one licence that travels. Many licences, issued faster.

A widespread misconception is that the compact resembles the Nurse Licensure Compact. It does not.

Under the nursing model, a single home-state licence confers privileges across all member states. Under the physicians' compact — and now the podiatric one — the applicant still ends up holding several discrete state licences. The compact simply expedites their issuance. Each licence is still issued by its own state board, governed by its own practice act, and renewed under its own fee schedule.

The applicant begins with a full, unrestricted licence in their home state.
They designate a State of Principal Licence, which verifies their credentials once.
The FPMB Compact Commission transmits that attestation to each target state.
Each target state issues its own licence under its own statute.
The applicant now holds several separate state licences, obtained substantially faster than the traditional pathway.

Jurisdiction is determined by patient location: a telehealth encounter with a patient in Iowa is governed by Iowa's board regardless of where the clinician sits. This is the legal principle that gives the compact its telemedicine value.

· · ·

Four facts that determine the rest.

Activation at four states.

Most contemporary healthcare compacts — the physician assistant compact, the dentist and dental hygienist compact, the counselling compact — require seven enacting states before they activate. The podiatric compact requires only four. The lower threshold reflects podiatry's smaller licensee base and FPMB's interest in standing up the commission quickly. It does not, however, change the practical fact that meaningful national reach still demands many more adoptions.

Eligibility, and the barrier residents miss.

The model legislation requires a CPME-approved school, all parts of the APMLE passed, a CPME-approved residency completed, an unrestricted home-state licence, a clean DEA record, an FBI biometric criminal-records check, and specialty board certification from ABFAS or ABPM. That final requirement is the one that excludes most new graduates. Board qualification after residency is not board certification; the certification pathway takes years of case logging and additional examinations. Until those are complete, the compact is unavailable.

Shared discipline, unshared scope.

Member boards exchange disciplinary and investigative information through the Commission's data system — a tighter regime than the present reliance on the National Practitioner Data Bank alone. But scope of practice remains local. A compact licensee in Florida may perform ankle surgery on a Florida patient and may not perform it on a Mississippi patient.

Fees that stack.

The Commission charges its own administrative fee. Each member state continues to charge its full licensure fee on top. For a clinician seeking only one or two additional states, traditional state-by-state licensure may still be the cheaper route.

Two of the four states needed to activate the compact have enacted it. The Commission has not been seated, and no compact licence has been issued anywhere. Two more adoptions stand between the compact and a working commission; a reasonable projection places functional operation one to three years away, and that assumes the next movers clear their chambers without a scope fight attached.

The compact itself is licensure, not scope — but state medical and orthopaedic societies do not always distinguish. The structural risk to adoption

Five misconceptions to discard.

Each is common; each will cost someone time or money if held to.

I will graduate from residency and use the compact.
Not immediately. Compact eligibility requires board certification, not board qualification. For most new graduates, the compact remains locked behind a two- to five-year wait after residency.
The compact harmonises scope of practice.
It does not. A compact licensee in Florida may perform ankle surgery on a Florida patient and may not perform it on a Mississippi patient. Scope remains a matter of local statute, and the compact takes no position on it.
It is essentially the nursing compact.
Structurally, no. Nurses receive a single licence that confers privileges across member states. Podiatrists receive several discrete licences, issued faster. The two compacts work on different principles.
APMA wrote it.
FPMB wrote it. APMA endorsed it and now carries it politically. The two bodies have different mandates and the distinction matters when one is asked to explain how the compact was developed.
It will be national within a year.
The physicians' compact took roughly a decade to reach forty jurisdictions. The podiatric compact has moved faster in its early stages but addresses a licensee population of perhaps eighteen thousand, against more than a million physicians. Many legislatures will not prioritise it.
· · ·

A decade behind, and why.

Podiatric medicine lagged the physician, nursing, physical therapy, and psychology compacts for the same combination of reasons that explains most things about its smaller political footprint. The licensee base is small. State-by-state scope battles consumed advocacy capacity for two decades. FPMB only matured into a true federation recently. And no federal funding stream existed for podiatric licensure portability until the HRSA Licensure Portability Grant Programme — a $750,000 award covering ninety-one per cent of project costs — was secured in 2024. The compact is what the profession's infrastructure finally produced once those four conditions changed at once.

· · ·

The political risk no one is naming.

In Mississippi in 2024, a podiatric scope-of-practice bill that referenced CPME 320 was characterised by the state medical society as a "compact-like" measure that would permit a national body to set surgical scope. A coalition of medical, orthopaedic, and surgery societies defeated it. That bill was technically about scope, not licensure — but the framing illustrates the structural risk for the IPMLC. In states with active scope-of-practice disputes, the podiatric compact may be opposed on grounds that have nothing to do with licensure portability. The adoption curve will be bumpy where those disputes are unresolved.

· · ·

For the resident and the student.

Six things worth doing, in roughly the order one should do them.

i.
Pursue board certification as early as possible. It is the eligibility gate, and there is no workaround.
ii.
Follow the state podiatric medical association rather than social media. The bill, the testimony, and the political coalition are at the state level.
iii.
Assume traditional licensure for the first one or two positions. The compact will not be functionally available in time.
iv.
If wide-scope training leads to a narrow-scope state, the compact will accelerate licensure but not expand what may lawfully be performed. The host state's practice act should be read before any contract is signed.
v.
Telehealth is the sleeper use case. Cross-state wound-care follow-up, diabetic-foot risk monitoring, and post-operative review are the activities most likely to be unlocked first.
vi.
For military spouses, the compact stacks with the Veterans Auto and Education Improvement Act of 2023. PCS-driven re-licensure becomes essentially frictionless in compact states.
