How Deep Should You
Microfracture?

What the literature actually says about awl depth, plate violation, and when to escalate.
By Peter Sorensen, DPM
⚡ BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT

The clinical convention is 2–4 mm awl depth, holes 3–4 mm apart — and that's what 81% of published series do. But the strongest controlled basic-science evidence says deeper, narrower perforations build better cartilage. No human RCT has ever compared depths head-to-head in the talus.

▼ Cross-Section: What Happens Below the Cartilage
Awls compact and "seal" bone around the channel; small-diameter drills leave the channel open.

Key Concepts

  • Steadman's original technique (1999): awls placed 3–4 mm apart, ~4 mm deep, into the subchondral bed until fat droplets or blood appear.
  • The "endpoint" is intra-op feedback, not measured depth — bleeding/fat = stop. This is what most surgeons actually do.
  • Diameter matters as much as depth. Smaller (≤1.0 mm) perforations preserve trabecular architecture; larger awls (≥2 mm) crush and compact bone.
  • The subchondral plate gets violated either way — the meaningful variable is the quality of disruption (sharp drill channels vs. crushed awl pits).
  • Lesion depth on MRI is prognostic. >7.8 mm lesion depth predicts worse outcomes. Cysts >5 mm push you toward augmentation.

Convention vs. Evidence

WHAT WE DO

2–4 mm Awl, 3–4 mm Spacing

Steadman 1999. Kok systematic review 2012 (6 studies, 198 patients) — 81% good/excellent outcomes. Powers/Dowd/Giza 2021 review reinforces the convention.

WHAT THE BENCH SAYS

1 mm Diameter, 6+ mm Deep

Chen/Hoemann (rabbit 2009, 2011): deeper drilling → more hyaline-character fill, more type II collagen, less type I. Eldracher/Madry (sheep 2014): 1.0 mm holes outperform 1.8 mm.

The disconnect: Clinical results at 2–4 mm awl depth are good enough that nobody has run a human RCT to test deeper drilling. The animal data have been around for 15 years.

When Lesion Depth Changes the Plan

▲ Lesion depth on preop MRI

Also consider: size >107 mm² (≈10.2 mm diameter), uncontained shoulder lesions, age >40, smoking — all push management away from isolated MFx. (Ramponi 2017 SR, Powers 2021).

depth <5 mm?
→
Isolated MFx is reasonable. Use a small (≤1 mm) awl, 2–4 mm deep, 3–4 mm apart. Stop at fat droplets.

→
Augment. AMIC, BioCartilage, or BMAC on top of MFx. Wang 2021 meta supports this.
Lesion >7.8 mm,
uncontained, or revision?
→
Graft. OAT, mosaicplasty, structural autograft, or osteochondral allograft.
High-demand athlete,
wants faster RTS?
→
Consider deeper, narrower (nanofracture or SPORT-style 1.2 mm × 10 mm drilling). Li 2026 RCT showed faster 3–6 mo recovery, equivalent at 1 yr.

⚠️ Pitfalls & Mistakes

  • Using a 2 mm awl by default. Cadaver micro-CT (Gianakos 2016) shows you're crushing more bone than you think — switch to a 1 mm awl or microdrill if available.
  • Treating "preserve the plate" as literal. You're penetrating it — that's the point. The goal is clean penetration, not avoidance.
  • Microfracturing large or deep lesions. >107 mm² area, >10 mm diameter, or >7.8 mm depth → expect failure. Escalate at the front end.
  • Ignoring postop subchondral BME on follow-up MRI. Persistent edema at 4 yr correlates with worse FAOS (Shimozono 2018). It's a signal, not noise.
  • Assuming all "augmented MFx" is equivalent. The Wang 2021 meta pools BMAC, PRP, HA, scaffolds — supports some augmentation, doesn't endorse a brand.

💎 Pearls

  • Endpoint > depth. Fat droplets and bleeding mean you're through. Don't chase a millimeter number on the awl.
  • Smaller diameter = better architecture. Reach for a 1 mm awl over a 2 mm awl whenever your tray allows.
  • Spacing matters. 3–4 mm between holes — keep the bridges intact, you need them for stability.
  • Calcified cartilage off, stable shoulders. Debridement quality predicts more than depth does.
  • Read the lesion before you read the tray. MRI depth + size determines technique selection. The awl is the last decision, not the first.

🔥 Controversies / Debates

  • Is deeper actually better in humans? Strong rabbit and sheep data. Zero human RCT comparing 2 mm vs 4 mm vs 6 mm in the talus. The translation step is missing.
  • Does the "sealing effect" matter clinically? 81% good outcomes at 2–4 mm awl depth suggests the compaction isn't catastrophic — but Shimozono's BME data suggest the substrate isn't healing as cleanly as we'd like.
  • Nanofracture vs MFx in the talus. Better histology on the bench, but Kasapoğlu 2025 RCT showed equivalent AOFAS at 12 months. Tissue ≠ symptoms — at least at 1 year.
  • The 7.8 mm cutoff. Widely cited from Powers 2021 / Choi WJ — but it's a soft threshold from prognostic series, not a hard rule from an RCT.

Current Trends (2024–2026)

  • Augmentation is the standard, not the exception for moderate-to-large OLT — Wang 2021 meta-analysis tipped this conversation.
  • Smaller-diameter tools winning the bench debate — nanofracture, SPORT-style 1.2 mm K-wires, microdrilling. Li/Jiang 2026 RCT is the strongest signal yet.
  • MRI substrate quality (MOCART, BME persistence) is replacing AOFAS as the more sensitive outcome measure for cartilage repair research.
  • Lesion-depth-based algorithms (rather than one-size-fits-all MFx) increasingly drive technique selection in foot & ankle consensus statements.
📊 Confidence Level: 7 / 10
High confidence on the 2–4 mm clinical convention and on the basic-science depth/diameter findings. Medium confidence on translating animal-model results to humans — no head-to-head depth RCT in the talus exists. ⚠️ Verify locally before applying.
