Why This History Matters
Podiatry is one of the few healthcare professions that can trace an unbroken line from ancient civilization to modern doctoral training and surgical subspecialization. Understanding that arc isn't trivia — it's professional identity. The scope battles, certification disputes, and legislative fights of today are the same story, different century. The DPMs who know their history can read the present more clearly.
Chapter 1 — Ancient Origins
3000 BC → 500 AD
The Ancient World
Foot Healers Before the Profession

Foot problems are as old as bipedal locomotion. Long before there was a word for podiatrist — or chiropodist, or corn-cutter — there were people whose purpose was to keep other people walking. The earliest documented evidence of this exists in stone.

≈ 2400 BC
The Tomb of Ankhmabor, Saqqara, Egypt
Among the most remarkable artifacts in the history of medicine: a wall relief in the tomb of a high Egyptian official depicts two practitioners treating the hands and feet of seated patients. The hieroglyphs are interpreted as the patient saying, "Do not hurt me" and the practitioner responding, "I shall act so you praise me." This is the oldest known depiction of medical foot care in recorded history.
≈ 400 BC
Hippocrates Describes Corns & Calluses
The Father of Medicine was among the first to formally document pathological foot conditions in clinical terms. He described corn and callus formation, attributed them to mechanical friction, and developed a manual scraping tool to remove hyperkeratotic tissue — the world's first documented podiatric instrument. His writings on foot conditions appear in the Hippocratic Corpus.
1st–4th Century AD
Roman Medicine & the Military Foot
Roman military medicine was sophisticated and practical — a standing army with foot problems was a strategic liability. Roman physicians developed rudimentary orthopedic footwear, and treatments for calluses, ulcers, and nail conditions were documented by physicians including Celsus and Galen.
Medieval Period
Barber-Surgeons & Corn-Cutters
In the absence of formal medical structures, foot care in medieval Europe fell primarily to barber-surgeons — the general practitioners of their era — and an informal class of itinerant "corn-cutters" who traveled town to town treating common foot ailments. This work was considered manual trade, not medicine, a distinction that would take centuries to reverse.
Chapter 2 — The Chiropodist Era
1700s → 1910s
Professionalization Begins
From Craft to Chiropodist

The word chiropodist — from Greek cheir (hand) and pous (foot) — entered the English language in the 18th century. This era marks the first serious attempt to define foot care as a distinct discipline with its own body of knowledge, practitioners, and eventually, educational standards.

1785
An Early English Chiropody Text: Chiropodologia
David Low, a London practitioner, published Chiropodologia: A Scientific Enquiry into the Causes of Corns, Warts, Bunions, and other Painful or Offensive Cutaneous Excrescences — an early formal text attempting to codify foot care as a discipline with anatomical and clinical basis. The attribution of Chiropodologia to David Low and its precise place in the lineage of English chiropody literature is debated among historians; the text is best understood as representative of a period in which foot care practitioners were beginning to assert a distinct professional identity in print.
Early 1800s
Royal Patronage Legitimizes the Field
European royal courts became unlikely validators of chiropodists' professional status. Nikolas Heaton served King George III; Victor Saux served Napoleon Bonaparte — treating the Emperor's notorious foot pain throughout the Napoleonic campaigns. Patronage by heads of state provided chiropodists with a social legitimacy that formal credentialing had not yet conferred.
1840s
Isaachar Zacharie — Lincoln's Chiropodist
Dr. Isaachar Zacharie served as chiropodist to President Abraham Lincoln during the Civil War. Lincoln reportedly praised him effusively — and notably used him as an informal diplomatic envoy to the Confederacy. Zacharie published early texts on foot disorders and is considered one of the first American chiropodists to argue for formal training requirements.
1895–1905
First Chiropody Schools Open in the U.S.
The first American chiropody schools opened in New York City, offering short-course training programs for foot care practitioners. Early programs were inconsistent in length, content, and rigor — a problem that would drive calls for standardization and eventually state-level licensure. The New York School of Chiropody (est. 1895) is among the earliest documented.
Historical Context
Why "Chiropodist" Fell Out of Use
The transition from "chiropodist" to "podiatrist" was a deliberate rebranding effort in the 20th century. The profession sought to:

(1) Shed associations with itinerant corn-cutters and barbers
(2) Align nomenclature with other recognized medical specialties (the -iatrist suffix mirrors psychiatrist, pediatrician)
(3) Strengthen the case for doctoral-level education and expanded scope of practice

The shift was gradual and contested — many practitioners preferred the original term well into the mid-20th century.

1912 — New York City
National Association of Chiropodists Founded
A group of practitioners gathered in New York to form the National Association of Chiropodists (NAC) — the organizational predecessor to today's APMA. The founding mission was immediate and practical: advocate for uniform licensing laws, establish educational standards, and distinguish trained chiropodists from untrained itinerant practitioners. This single act marks the birth of American organized podiatry.
1915
First State Licensure: New York Leads
New York became the first state to enact formal licensure requirements for chiropodists, establishing legal recognition that practitioners must meet defined educational and examination standards. State-by-state campaigns followed over subsequent decades — a fragmented process that created lasting variation in scope-of-practice laws that persists in modified form today.
1941
The DPM Degree Established
The Doctor of Podiatric Medicine (DPM) degree emerged from this era, representing a watershed shift away from diploma-mill certificates toward a recognized doctoral credential. The transition required alignment of curriculum, clinical training hours, and examination standards — signaling that foot care had reached the threshold of a recognized medical specialty. Note: The exact date of formal DPM degree establishment is cited variably in the literature; 1941 represents a commonly cited transitional milestone but should be verified against primary institutional records before publication.
Chapter 4 — War Elevates the Profession
1917 → 1975
Military Medicine
How Warfare Shaped Podiatric Medicine

Each major American military conflict of the 20th century advanced the professional standing of podiatry — not because of politics, but because war is hard on feet. The tactical cost of foot disease forced military planners to take podiatry seriously, and that recognition translated into expanded scope and institutional legitimacy.

WWI · 1917
Trench Foot — The First Mass Podiatric Emergency
Trench foot — a non-freezing cold injury caused by prolonged exposure to cold, wet conditions — incapacitated hundreds of thousands of Allied soldiers. The British Army alone documented over 20,000 cases. The crisis demonstrated with undeniable clarity that foot disease could decide battles. Military medicine began formally incorporating podiatric practitioners into treatment protocols.
WWII · 1941
Podiatrists Commissioned as Military Officers
WWII saw podiatric physicians serving in military medical roles, with foot readiness recognized as a command priority. Soldiers with untreated foot conditions had measurably reduced combat effectiveness. The institutional elevation of foot care within military medicine during this period is well documented, though the precise commissioning structures and deployment scope of podiatric personnel varied and deserve verification against military medical records.
Korea/Vietnam
Surgical Scope Expansion Under Combat Conditions
The complex traumatic foot and ankle injuries sustained in Korea and Vietnam demanded more than routine foot care. Post-war, veterans with service-connected foot injuries drove significant demand for podiatric surgical care — and provided a practical justification for the expansion of civilian surgical training programs that accelerated through the 1960s and 1970s.
Post-WWII
The VA System Expands Podiatric Access
The Veterans Administration healthcare system became a major institutional home for podiatric practice in the postwar decades. VA hospitals provided DPMs with access to hospital infrastructure, surgical facilities, and complex patient populations — accelerating clinical training and legitimizing inpatient podiatric care long before many private hospitals granted comparable privileges.
Chapter 5 — The Surgical Revolution
1960s → 1990s
Entering the OR
How Podiatry Became a Surgical Specialty

The transformation of podiatry from a primarily office-based, nonsurgical specialty into a recognized surgical discipline is one of the most significant — and most contested — chapters in the profession's history. It unfolded over three decades of policy fights, hospital privilege battles, residency program development, and eventually, board certification.

Early 1960s
Hospital Privileges: The First Fights
The early 1960s saw the first systematic efforts by podiatric surgeons to gain operating room privileges at U.S. hospitals. Resistance was intense — from hospital medical staffs, MD surgeons, and insurance carriers. Victories were institution-by-institution, often requiring years of negotiation, credentialing documentation, and political capital. Privilege denials were common. Many DPMs performed surgeries in outpatient surgical suites or office settings outside hospital systems entirely.
1975
American Board of Podiatric Surgery Founded
The American Board of Podiatric Surgery (ABPS) — now the American Board of Foot and Ankle Surgery (ABFAS) — was established to create a formal board certification pathway for podiatric surgeons. This was a critical legitimizing step: it established that podiatric surgical training could be assessed, verified, and certified by an independent examining body using defined competency standards.
1970s
Structured Surgical Residency Programs Established
The 1970s saw the widespread development of structured podiatric surgical residency programs — post-doctoral training placements in hospital settings that provided supervised operative experience. This was transformational: before residency, surgical training was largely self-directed or apprenticeship-based. Standardized post-doctoral training is what makes a profession, and the residency model was the necessary infrastructure for the surgical era.
1970s–80s
The Scope Battles: Ankle & Leg Inclusion
As surgical competency grew, so did the profession's ambitions — and the legislative resistance. State-by-state scope-of-practice battles were fought over whether DPMs could legally operate on the ankle, distal leg, and associated structures. These fights were not purely clinical — they were economic and political turf wars with orthopedics and general surgery. State statutes remain non-uniform to this day as a direct result.
1980s–90s
Complex Reconstruction: Total Ankle, Arthroscopy, Flatfoot
By the late 1980s and through the 1990s, podiatric surgeons were performing and advancing techniques in total ankle arthroplasty, ankle arthroscopy, Achilles tendon reconstruction, and complex flatfoot correction — procedures once exclusively within orthopedic territory. The surgical complexity of podiatric practice was no longer theoretical; it was documented in peer-reviewed literature and surgical outcome data.
"Podiatric surgical scope was not legislated into existence — it was demonstrated into existence. Hospital by hospital, outcome by outcome, the data made the argument that credentials alone could not."
— Sole Intelligence editorial

Est. 1918
CPME
Council on Podiatric Medical Education
The CPME was established in 1918 as the Council on Education and began accrediting colleges of podiatric medicine in 1922 — making it one of the oldest specialty accrediting bodies in U.S. healthcare. Currently accredits 11 DPM programs, including newer additions LECOM and UT Rio Grande Valley. CPME's 2011 implementation of the single three-year residency requirement was a landmark shift in post-doctoral training rigor.
Est. 1912 / Renamed 1984
APMA
American Podiatric Medical Association
The primary professional organization and legislative advocate for podiatric medicine. Founded as the National Association of Chiropodists in 1912, renamed the American Podiatry Association in 1957, and given its current name in 1984. The APMA's House of Delegates is the governing legislative body of organized podiatry.
Est. 1975 / Renamed ABFAS 2014
ABFAS
American Board of Foot & Ankle Surgery
The surgical board certifying body for podiatric physicians. Offers board qualification (ABFAS-Q) and board certification (ABFAS-C) in rearfoot/ankle surgery and forefoot/soft tissue surgery. Board certification is not required for licensure but carries significant weight in hospital credentialing and practice reputation.
Est. 1978
ABPM
American Board of Podiatric Medicine
The second certifying board, focused on podiatric medicine and primary care rather than surgery. The existence of two certifying boards — ABFAS and ABPM — reflects a longstanding internal debate about whether podiatry is primarily a surgical specialty, a primary care specialty, or both. This tension is alive in current "One Board" discussions.

Chapter 7 — Organized Podiatry & the APMA
1912 → Present
Institutional History
The APMA: 100 Years of Organized Advocacy
1912
National Association of Chiropodists — New York City
Founded in New York by practitioners seeking unified advocacy, uniform licensing standards, and professional separation from unlicensed corn-cutters. First formal governance structure for American foot care practitioners.
1957
Renamed: American Podiatry Association
The name change from "chiropodist" to "podiatrist" reflected the profession's alignment with doctoral-level training and its aspiration toward parity with other recognized medical specialties. The 1957 rename followed a major NAC reorganization in 1956 with a new constitution and governance structure.
1984
Renamed: American Podiatric Medical Association
Addition of "Medical" to the name was a deliberate positioning choice — emphasizing the full clinical scope of podiatric practice beyond surgery or manual care. The APMA's House of Delegates became the primary legislative body, meeting annually to vote on policy resolutions affecting the profession.
2012
Centennial Year: 100 Years of Organized Podiatry
The APMA's 100th anniversary marked a century of organized professional advocacy — from a group of New York chiropodists fighting licensure battles to a national organization representing 18,000+ DPMs navigating scope legislation, Medicare policy, and graduate medical education reform.
2026 — Washington, D.C.
100th APMA President Inaugurated: Dr. Patrick DeHeer, DPM
At the 2026 APMA House of Delegates, Dr. Patrick DeHeer, DPM was inaugurated as the 100th President of the APMA — a milestone presidency in a milestone year. Dr. DeHeer is an Indianapolis-based surgeon and nationally recognized figure in podiatric surgery, education, and advocacy.
Chapter 8 — The Certification Wars
1975 → Present
Internal Power Structures
ABFAS vs. ABPM: The Two-Board Tension

One of the most consequential and least publicly understood fault lines in podiatric medicine is the existence of two separate certifying boards with different philosophies, different exam structures, and different visions for what a fully trained DPM should be.

ABFAS — Surgical Track
  • Est. 1975 (as ABPS; renamed ABFAS 2014)
  • Certifies in Rearfoot/Ankle and Forefoot/Soft Tissue Surgery
  • Requires documented surgical case logs for board qualification
  • Requires residency completion prior to candidacy
  • Weighted toward surgical case volume and complexity
  • Historically associated with the surgical identity of the profession
ABPM — Medicine Track
  • Est. 1978
  • Certifies in Podiatric Medicine (non-surgical primary care)
  • Emphasizes medical management, biomechanics, and wound care
  • Pathway more accessible to non-surgical practitioners
  • Associated with primary care identity and internal medicine alignment
  • Some advocate this model for ACGME transition compatibility
Active Debate
The "One Board" Resolution
Advocates for a unified certification structure argue that two boards create confusion among hospital credentialing committees, insurance carriers, and the public about what a board-certified DPM actually means. The "One Board" proposal — periodically surfacing in APMA House of Delegates resolutions — would merge ABFAS and ABPM into a single certifying body with a unified examination. Opponents argue this would either dilute surgical standards or exclude non-surgical practitioners. The debate remains unresolved and politically charged.
Chapter 9 — The ACGME Question
2000s → Present
Graduate Medical Education
The Most Important Structural Fight of the Modern Era

The question of whether podiatric residency programs should transition from CPME accreditation to ACGME accreditation is the defining institutional battle of contemporary podiatric medicine. The stakes are enormous — and the outcome will shape the profession's identity for generations.

Arguments FOR ACGME Transition
  • Aligns DPM training with MD/DO GME infrastructure
  • Improves parity in hospital credentialing and privileges
  • Potential Medicare GME funding access for ACGME programs
  • Strengthens scope-of-practice arguments in state legislatures
  • Perceived competitive advantage in hiring and hospital affiliation
  • Signals commitment to mainstream medical education standards
Arguments AGAINST ACGME Transition
  • ACGME may not accommodate podiatric-specific surgical training needs
  • Risk of losing CPME autonomy and podiatric identity in training
  • Potential reduction in surgical case volume under ACGME structures
  • CPME programs can be restructured without full ACGME transition
  • No clear evidence ACGME programs produce better DPM surgeons
  • Transition costs and accreditation process are resource-intensive
What's Actually Happening
A Hybrid Model Is Emerging
Rather than a wholesale transition, the profession is navigating a hybrid landscape where national organizations debate dual accreditation pathways and some institutions explore ACGME alignment — but no programs are currently operating under full dual CPME/ACGME accreditation. The APMA HOD has passed resolutions exploring the question. The practical outcome for graduating residents in the near term: CPME-accredited programs remain fully recognized for licensure and board eligibility in all 50 states.
Chapter 10 — The Modern Era
2000s → Present
Technology · Scope · AI
Where Podiatry Stands Today
🖨️
3D Printing & Custom Orthotics
CAD/CAM and additive manufacturing have transformed orthotic fabrication — custom foot orthoses now designed digitally and printed in hours, replacing manual casting and weeks-long lab turnaround. Gait analysis integration with pressure mapping produces data-driven prescriptions impossible a decade ago.
🔬
Minimally Invasive Surgery (MIS)
Percutaneous and keyhole techniques have redefined recovery expectations for bunion, hammertoe, and flatfoot correction. MIS is rapidly becoming a core competency expectation in modern podiatric surgical training — its adoption is accelerating, though standardization across programs remains uneven.
🧬
Biologics & Regenerative Medicine
Platelet-rich plasma, amniotic allografts, stem cell applications, and bioengineered wound matrices have expanded the podiatric toolkit for diabetic foot ulcer management, tendon repair, and post-surgical healing — areas where DPMs are often the primary specialist.
🤖
AI & Clinical Decision Support
Artificial intelligence is entering podiatric practice — from LLM-assisted billing and coding to diabetic foot ulcer imaging analysis, gait classification, and AI-augmented surgical planning. This is the most disruptive technological shift since the introduction of fluoroscopy and remains largely unstandardized in podiatric education.
⚖️
Scope of Practice: Still Unfinished Business
Despite a century of legislative progress, DPM scope of practice remains non-uniform across states. The inclusion of the ankle and distal leg in surgical scope, independent prescriptive authority, and hospital medical staff membership rights continue to be fought in state legislatures — the same fundamental battles, different political terrain.
