⚡ The Long View

How Pop Media Learned to Love the Foot Doctor.

A century of depictions — from vaudeville joke to TLC surgeon, from Seinfeld's punching bag to TikTok's 8-million-follower foot surgeon — and what each era reveals about how the public sees our profession.

By Peter Sorensen, DPM · For The Guidewire

What This Is

A comprehensive survey of how podiatrists have been portrayed in film, television, literature, and digital media — and why those portrayals shifted.

Why It Matters

Cultural depictions shape referral patterns, student interest, patient expectations, and professional identity. Every pop-culture joke is a recruiting obstacle or an opportunity.

01 The Timeline Podiatrists on screen
1900s–2020s
1904–1968

The Dr. Scholl Era

Commerce / Advertising

Before podiatrists appeared as characters, they appeared as brands. William M. Scholl — Chicago chiropodist, Illinois College of Chiropody founder (1912), and master of direct marketing — dressed in winged collars and tailcoats and toured shoe stores with a foot skeleton in his pocket. His "When your feet hurt, you hurt all over" slogan and annual Foot Comfort Week made the chiropodist-as-authority figure a household image for half a century. Scholl never reached the public as a doctor in dramatic fiction. He reached them as a logo.

"The man who made the world foot-conscious." — Time magazine, on Scholl's death in 1968 Brand recognition, credential ambiguity
1970s

The Borscht Belt Years

Stand-up / Sitcom

Through the 1970s the podiatrist existed almost exclusively as a setup: shorthand for a minor doctor, the specialty you could joke about without controversy. Bunions, corns, and in-grown nails were reliable punchlines on variety shows and early sitcoms. No named, recurring podiatrist character of cultural significance emerges from the decade. This isn't neutral absence — it's the era in which the frame ("not a real doctor") was being culturally laid down, quietly, before any fictional podiatrist ever had to carry it.

The punchline infrastructure
1993

Seinfeld, "The Conversion" (S5, E11)

NBC / Sitcom

The most consequential three-line exchange in podiatry's cultural history. Elaine is dating a podiatrist. Jerry is unimpressed.

ELAINE: "Jerry, the man is a doctor."
JERRY: "Doctor? He's a podiatrist."
ELAINE: "It's the same thing."
JERRY: "Anyone can get into podiatry school. George got into podiatry school." — Bruce Kirschbaum, Seinfeld, Dec. 16, 1993

The episode's podiatrist character is flat and forgettable; the joke, however, entered the bloodstream. Three decades later, podiatry students and residents still get asked "are you a real doctor?" and the question traces, in a straight line, to this scene. Notably: Jerry Seinfeld himself later donated twice to the New York College of Podiatric Medicine, funding a gait lab and a symposium. The profession's relationship to the joke is complicated.

The line that will not die
2005

Curb Your Enthusiasm, "The Christ Nail"

HBO / Sitcom

Larry David — Seinfeld's co-creator — returns to the well. A podiatrist (played by Carlos Jacott) appears briefly, mostly as a plot mechanic. The character is less insulted than indifferently used, which is arguably worse: the specialty is now background furniture, a profession that exists to make a story happen and then leave the scene. The comedic frame is identical to Seinfeld's; only the intensity has faded.

From insult to irrelevance
2010s

The Background-Character Years

Scripted TV, various

Across prestige cable and streaming drama, podiatrists appear almost exclusively as connective tissue: the character whose office someone visits, whose specialty is mentioned in dialogue, whose presence allows a plot to move. Not heroes, not villains, not even really satirized — just present. In an era when dermatologists got Emmy-bait storylines (Nip/Tuck) and orthopedic surgeons anchored hospital dramas (Grey's Anatomy), podiatry remained dramatically invisible. The absence itself is the data.

Dramatic invisibility
2019–2020

The TLC Reset

Reality TV

The pivotal shift. Riding the success of Dr. Pimple Popper, TLC commissions a slate of foot-doctor reality series. The Toe Bro (A&E, 2019) features Toronto chiropodist Jonathan Tomines. Dr. Foot Fixer (TLC, 2019) centers podiatrist Yolanda Ragland. My Feet Are Killing Me (TLC, January 2020) pairs Drs. Brad Schaeffer and Ebonie Vincent, later adding Dr. Sarah Haller — the first sustained portrayal of podiatric surgeons as protagonists in American television. For the first time, mass audiences watched podiatrists perform reconstructive surgery, limb salvage, and diabetic wound care. The frame shifts from "not a real doctor" to "these people are saving lives with power tools."

"It was clear, following the debut of Dr. Pimple Popper, that our audience was craving even more powerful and heartwarming stories of medical transformation." — Howard Lee, then-GM of TLC, 2019

Sometimes the shows lean toward the grotesque — extreme fungus, overgrown nails, gruesome wounds — maybe rebranding podiatry as the gross-out specialty. But the surgical footage is real, the outcomes are real, and for millions of viewers this is the first time they see what podiatric surgery actually looks like.

Protagonist status achieved
2019–Now

The FootDocDana Phenomenon

TikTok / YouTube / Instagram

Dr. Dana Figura (DPM, @footdocdana) begins posting on TikTok in November 2019 as a podiatric resident in Los Angeles. By 2024 her combined following across TikTok, YouTube, and Instagram exceeds 8 million. Her "Doctor Reacts: Shaving HUGE Foot Callus" video has drawn over 54 million views. She is, by audience size, the most visible podiatrist who has ever lived. She is not alone: dozens of DPMs now run substantial educational accounts. The podiatrist-as-influencer is no longer an oddity; it is a career path.

Podiatry, finally, at scale
2024

The Bad Foot Clinic (UK)

Channel 5 / Reality

British podiatrist Marion Yau and GP husband Dr. Kenny anchor a UK analog to the TLC model — the format has now become international template. The reality foot-doctor show is no longer a novelty; it's a genre with predictable beats: anxious patient, dramatic reveal, graphic procedure, emotional resolution. Whatever its aesthetic problems, the genre's existence certifies that podiatric content reliably draws audience.

Genre maturity
02 The Four Archetypes Every pop-culture
podiatrist is one of these
I.
The Lesser Doctor

The Punchline

The podiatrist as shorthand for a watered-down physician. Their medical status is the joke. Usually nameless, rarely seen treating patients. Exists to let a "real" doctor look credentialed by contrast.

ex. Seinfeld S5E11 · stand-up · sitcom asides
II.
Connective Tissue

The Plot Device

A podiatrist appears because a story needs a reason for characters to be somewhere or to learn something. The specialty is incidental. Not insulted — just used and dismissed.

ex. Curb "The Christ Nail" · procedurals
III.
The Shock-Effect TV Protagonist

The Reality Surgeon

Real DPMs centered as show leads. Frame: "extraordinary problems, heroic intervention." Surgical work is real.

ex. My Feet Are Killing Me · The Toe Bro
IV.
The Native Media Era

The Creator-Educator

Podiatrists who built their own audience platforms and route around traditional media gatekeepers. Controls tone, frame, and message directly. The first archetype where the profession speaks for itself.

ex. FootDocDana · (The Guidewire)
03 Key Concepts How to read
a depiction
Concept 01
Legitimacy Framing

Does the depiction treat the podiatrist as a physician, or position them adjacent to medicine? This is the single most consequential variable — it shapes referral behavior and patient trust.

Concept 02
Scope Visibility

What does the depiction show the podiatrist doing? Nail care and orthotics read as technician-level work although these are important aspects of holistic patient care. Surgical reconstruction, limb salvage, and trauma read as specialist medicine.

Concept 03
Authorial Control

Who is shaping the frame — an outside writer, a reality producer, or the podiatrist themselves? Owned-channel content (TikTok, Substack) is the first medium where the profession isn't a supporting character.

Concept 04
Genre Conventions

Sitcom needs a punchline. Prestige drama needs an antagonist or sage. Reality TV needs a transformation. Each genre pre-shapes what a podiatrist character can be before a writer types a single word.

Concept 05
The Scholl Shadow

A century of OTC foot-care advertising trained the public to associate foot health with consumer products — not with physicians. Podiatry has always competed with the drugstore aisle for mindshare.

Concept 06
Absence as Signal

Podiatry is nearly invisible in prestige medical drama. That absence is not benign — it shapes which specialties pre-med students imagine as aspirational.

04 Pitfalls & Pearls For clinicians
reading the culture
Pitfalls
  • Dismissing pop culture as frivolous. The "not a real doctor" question has demonstrable downstream effects on scope battles, hospital privileges, and medical-student recruiting.
  • Treating reality TV uncritically. The gross-out frame is not neutral. It trades on disgust to hold attention, and that framing follows the profession off-screen.
  • Waiting for Hollywood to get it right. Thirty years of evidence say the industry isn't coming to rescue us. The frame won't change until someone else controls the camera.
  • Ignoring the creator economy. A single DPM with 8M followers now out-reaches every fictional podiatrist character in TV history, combined.
Pearls
  • The protagonist era has already begun. Since 2019, there have been more substantive podiatric depictions than in the preceding 50 years combined.
  • Authorial control is the asset. Substack, YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram allow DPMs to show surgical scope, academic rigor, and clinical outcomes without a producer deciding what's interesting.
  • Show the surgery. Public misunderstanding of podiatric scope is near-total. Footage of real cases — reconstructive, trauma, limb salvage — reframes the specialty faster than any op-ed.
  • The Seinfeld joke is retireable. It survived because no competing cultural frame existed. One is now being built, in real time, by podiatrists themselves.
05 Where This Is Going The next decade

Every specialty has a story the public tells about it. Cardiology's story is urgency. Neurosurgery's is genius. Dermatology's, post-Nip/Tuck and post-Dr. Pimple Popper, is transformation. Podiatry's public story has been, for most of a century, absence punctuated by minor ridicule.

That is changing — not because Hollywood learned to take us seriously, but because the distribution of storytelling has changed. The same technological shift that let a Los Angeles podiatric resident become a household name on TikTok has opened space for dozens of DPMs to teach, educate, and frame the profession on their own terms.

The pop-culture podiatrist of the 1990s was a stranger's joke. The pop-culture podiatrist of the 2020s is increasingly an actual podiatrist, talking about actual work, to an audience the profession built itself. The next generation of residents and students will enter a field whose public story is being written, for the first time, by its own practitioners.

That is the opportunity — and for those of us working at the intersection of podiatry and public communication, it is also the obligation.
