Professional literacy

Podiatry's alphabet soup problem.

What 129 trainees told us about the institutions shaping their profession, and the simple on-ramps that could change engagement.

Peter Sorensen, DPM August 9, 2026 8 min read
The short version The profession already has many of the programs trainees need. The missing layer is a clear map and a meaningful first role.
Based on the Professional Literacy in Podiatric Medicine Survey, June-July 2026. Unpublished data, n=129.

You cannot engage what you cannot name. That sentence began as the opening line of our APMA Emerging Leaders Program capstone. After presenting the work to the APMA Board of Trustees and the ELP audience at The National, it felt less like a presentation hook and more like a diagnosis.

Podiatric trainees move through a profession dense with organizations, councils, boards, associations, and acronyms. These institutions accredit schools and residencies, certify physicians, set policy, advocate for scope and reimbursement, and create leadership pathways. Yet we rarely teach the map as a map.

If residents experience the profession as alphabet soup, belonging becomes accidental.

We wanted to know whether this was just a communication complaint or a measurable literacy gap. So we stopped guessing and built a national survey.

The survey paired self-rated familiarity with tested knowledge. Acronyms were free recall, not multiple choice. Governance items tested concepts such as accreditation, certification, advocacy, and the APMA House of Delegates.

Before reading further, try the room test we used during the presentation. Without looking anything up, write out these four acronyms in full:

The gap is real, and seniority did not close it.

Only 45% of respondents could correctly write out CPME, the Council on Podiatric Medical Education. Forty-six percent could write out NBPME. Only 20% could expand ACGME. Recognition would probably have produced higher scores, but recognition was not the question. We wanted to know whether trainees could retrieve the names of institutions that shape their training.

More time in training did not reliably teach the system. Mean knowledge was 70% among MS-3 respondents and 58% among the PGY-4+, fellow, and first-year-in-practice group. Those endpoint figures should not be read as a longitudinal decline because the survey did not follow the same people over time. The narrower conclusion is the important one: seniority did not produce a consistent rise in professional literacy.

The trainees are willing. The curriculum is not there.

The strongest finding was not low recall. It was the mismatch between demand and delivery.

77%want more structured education on organized podiatry.

The audience already exists.

Eighty-one percent said this knowledge is very or extremely important. Yet only 36% reported formal curriculum exposure, and just 16% felt their program prepared them very well.

This is not apathy. Only 5% selected “I do not see the value” as their main barrier to engagement. Trainees are not rejecting the profession. They are asking the profession to explain itself.

“More standardization and less alphabet soup.”
MS-4 survey respondent

Engagement and knowledge moved together.

Respondents who had attended more than one organized-podiatry event averaged 79% on the knowledge assessment. Those who had never attended averaged 62%, a 17-point difference.

That association does not prove that attending events caused higher scores. More knowledgeable trainees may be more likely to attend. Still, the pattern matters because it points toward a reinforcing loop: early exposure makes the system more legible, legibility makes participation easier, and participation creates the relationships that turn membership into belonging.

The barrier data sharpen the strategy. During training, time dominates. Cost barely registers. Twenty-two percent simply do not know how to get involved, and 74% prefer email or social updates. That sounds less like a demand for another benefit and more like a request for a short, useful, consistent on-ramp.

Teach it. Package it. Seat them.

APMA already maintains a broad member-benefit catalog, including the Resident Essentials Bundle, student-loan guidance, the Young Physician Transition Series, a Career Center, advocacy resources, and leadership programs. The strategic problem is not that nothing exists. It is that a trainee can pass through residency without receiving a coherent map of what exists, who governs what, and where their own voice belongs.

Teach it

Put professional literacy inside residency academics. Use a short, standardized session that makes APMA, CPME, NBPME, ABFAS, ABPM, ACFAS, and ACGME distinct. Teach the House of Delegates as the association's legislative and governing body, not as an annual email.

Package it

Give every PGY-1 one Resident Quick-Start at orientation. Organize benefits around real moments: loans, education, protection, advocacy, research, job search, contracts, and transition to practice. One page. One channel worth opening. One clear action.

Seat them

Create visible, consequential resident roles in national governance, state associations, committees, task forces, and scientific programs. Representation should be designed into the system, not offered only when an individual leader remembers to invite someone.

The capstone team's presentation reported a 2026 House action creating a resident and fellow representation pathway. That is a meaningful opening, but a seat only matters when trainees understand the room, know how to enter it, and see that their work changes something.

A practical five-part pilot

  1. Charter a resident outreach task force.Make it resident-led, accountable, and connected to an existing APMA committee that can act on its work.
  2. Pilot the Resident Quick-Start at 10 programs.Deliver it during PGY-1 orientation and test whether knowledge, resource use, and participation change.
  3. Build durable resident representation.Support national participation, mirror it with state-association resident liaisons, and publish a clear route into service.
  4. Place a resident on every task force.Turn representation into standing policy rather than a personality-dependent gesture.
  5. Lower the cost of resident science.Offer research travel support and reduce open-access publication fees for resident first authors.

This pilot is intentionally modest. The infrastructure largely exists. The open question is ownership: who is responsible for making sure every trainee receives the map, the first invitation, and a role that matters?

Professional literacy is retention infrastructure.

A resident who understands organized podiatry can evaluate its value, use its resources, challenge its decisions, and eventually lead it. Literacy comes first. Engagement follows. Belonging has a chance to last.
