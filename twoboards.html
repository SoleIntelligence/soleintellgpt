01 · What This Is
The Core Problem
Podiatry is one of the only doctoral-level health professions with two separate, competing certifying boards — each with different standards, different pathways, and strong institutional incentives to survive independently.
"One profession. Two boards. Infinite confusion — for hospitals, insurers, trainees, and the public."

02 · Why It Matters
For Residents
Which board do you sit for? Does it matter for your job? Hospital privileges? The answer changes depending on where you practice.
For the Profession
A fractured system weakens podiatry's case for ACGME accreditation, scope expansion, and parity with other surgical specialties.
For Hospitals
Credentialers don't know what to do with two boards. Some facilities require both. Others recognize neither. Privileging confusion is real.
For Patients
No unified standard = no clear signal of competency. Public trust suffers when the profession can't agree on what "board-certified" means.
03 · The Two Boards
ABFAS
American Board of Foot and Ankle Surgery
→ Certifies in Forefoot Surgery and Reconstructive Rearfoot/Ankle (RRA)
→ Aligned with surgical residency training
→ Descended from ABPS (est. 1975)
→ Preferred by most hospital credentialing committees
→ Pathway: PMSR → Case logs → Written + Oral boards
vs
ABPM
American Board of Podiatric Medicine
→ Certifies in Podiatric Medicine — non-surgical and medical scope
→ Aligned with primary care–style podiatry
→ Descended from ABPOPPM, renamed 2006
→ Offers CAQs in subspecialty areas
→ Pathway: Residency → Written exam → Practice review
⚡ The Structural Problem
Both boards are independent corporations with their own bylaws, revenue, and exam fees. Neither reports to the other. Neither is subordinate to APMA. This makes consolidation a political negotiation — not a unilateral decision.
04 · Historical Timeline
1912
Profession founded. Schools of chiropody established in the U.S. Narrow scope — nails, calluses, routine care. No formalized board certification.
1975
ABPS established. The American Board of Podiatric Surgery becomes the first formal surgical certifying body — a major milestone for surgical credibility.
1980s–90s
A second board emerges. ABPOPPM forms to certify the non-surgical side of practice. The profession now runs on two parallel credentialing tracks.
1993
CPME redefines residency. Three-year PMSR/RRA programs become standard, aligning training with surgical scope — but credentialing remains split between two boards.
2006
ABPOPPM becomes ABPM. The rebranding signals a deliberate "podiatric medicine" identity separate from the surgical track.
2010s
ACGME conversation heats up. As podiatry eyes ACGME accreditation, the two-board structure becomes a visible liability — ACGME specialties typically operate under one unified board.
2013–15
One Board discussions begin formally. APMA HOD receives resolutions directing APMA to study consolidation. Task forces form; reports are commissioned.
2018–19
HOD authorizes formal merger talks. Task force empowered to negotiate with both boards. Progress is slow — both institutions resist ceding authority and revenue.
2020–22
COVID pause + CAQ controversy. ABPM pursues Certificates of Added Qualification. ABFAS and much of organized podiatry view this as scope expansion without consensus, further straining inter-board relations.
2023
Task Force reactivated. APMA formally reconstitutes the One Board Task Force with ABFAS, ABPM, CPME, program directors, and resident representation.
2024
HOD receives interim reports. Debate shifts to: full merger vs. joint governance model. State delegations with strong ABPM presence push back on surgical-dominant proposals.
2025–26
ABPM CAQ vote + active task force. CAQ categories (wound care, sports medicine) become a HOD flashpoint. Task force final report anticipated. Unification remains unresolved.
05 · The Debate
✓ Arguments FOR One Board
→ Cleaner hospital privileging
→ Stronger ACGME accreditation case
→ Unified public-facing identity
→ Fewer exam fees for trainees
→ Eliminates employer/insurer confusion
→ Aligns with every ACGME specialty model
→ More negotiating leverage with CMS
✗ Obstacles & Objections
→ ABPM fears loss of identity & autonomy
→ ABFAS fears dilution of surgical standards
→ CAQ holders' investment may be devalued
→ Both boards lose revenue/fee streams
→ No legal mechanism to compel merger
→ APMA HOD resolutions are non-binding
→ Medical-scope states resist surgical dominance
⚡ The Real Subtext
This isn't just a credentialing debate — it's a scope-of-practice proxy war. ABFAS represents a surgically dominant model of podiatry. ABPM represents a broader medical model. Neither wants to be the absorbed party. The board debate is how that identity conflict gets fought.

⚖️ HOD Power — Reality Check
The APMA HOD can direct APMA leadership and pass resolutions — but it cannot legally compel ABFAS or ABPM to merge. Both are independent corporations. HOD resolutions are politically powerful but structurally non-binding on the boards themselves.

📋 The Internal Tension
ABPM delegates push for any final board to include a medical/non-surgical track. ABFAS delegates and residency directors push for a surgery-centered model that mirrors ACGME specialty boards. Neither side wants to be the absorbed party.
⏳ Current Status (2025–2026)
The task force is active. Final recommendations remain unratified. The ABPM CAQ controversy has complicated negotiations — many in organized podiatry view new CAQs as ABPM expanding its leverage right before a merger could reduce it. Resolution at the 2026 HOD cycle remains uncertain.

✅

❌
Non-surgical / medical-scope DPMs — if the unified board is surgery-dominant, their credentialing pathway may be devalued or narrowed
❌
Existing CAQ holders — ABPM CAQs may lose recognition or value under a reorganized single-board structure
⚡

⚡

09 · Common Misconceptions
⚠️
"APMA can just order the boards to merge." — No. APMA can pass resolutions and apply political pressure, but both boards are legally independent. Consolidation requires voluntary agreement from both.
⚠️
"ABFAS makes ABPM irrelevant." — In many surgical practices, yes. But certain employers, states, and payer panels still recognize ABPM. Ignoring it can cost you credentialing opportunities.
⚠️
"The task force will solve this soon." — This has been in motion since the 2010s. Institutional inertia, conflicting financial interests, and no binding enforcement mechanism make rapid resolution unlikely.
⚠️
"This doesn't affect residents." — It directly affects which exams you sit for, how hospitals credential you, how your scope is perceived, and whether your program eventually moves under ACGME.
10 · Pearls
💎
The board debate is a proxy for a deeper identity question: Is podiatry a surgical specialty, a primary care foot specialty, or both? The answer to that drives everything downstream.
💎
Watch the ABPM CAQ fight closely: How organized podiatry handles CAQ expansion is a real-time test of whether the profession can self-regulate before ACGME or CMS imposes structure externally.
💎
ACGME accreditation is the forcing function: If podiatry achieves ACGME accreditation, a unified board will likely be structurally required — compressing the consolidation timeline dramatically.
💎
Know your state and employer before choosing boards: Until there's one board, credentialing requirements vary. Research what your target hospitals and payer networks actually recognize.
💎
Residents and young DPMs will close this debate: Engagement in APMA organized structure — state societies, HOD delegate roles — is how the generation most affected finally resolves this.
